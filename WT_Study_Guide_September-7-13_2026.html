Introduction & Act With Faith and Humility
1-2. Why should we be interested in what the Bible says about the Gibeonites?

In 1473 B.C.E., right after Israel's stunning victories over Jericho and Ai, a group of travelers showed up at the Israelite camp claiming to be from a far-off land and asking for a peace treaty. This is our first introduction to the Gibeonites in Scripture, but far from the last — the Bible traces their story across many centuries, and each account reveals something valuable about Jehovah's own personality as well as practical lessons for us today.

💬 Comment you could share: I found it interesting that the Bible doesn't just mention the Gibeonites once — their story keeps resurfacing across hundreds of years, almost like Jehovah wanted us to have multiple angles on the same lessons.
🙏 Getting to know Jehovah: Even a "minor" nation like the Gibeonites gets extended, careful attention in the Bible record — a reminder that Jehovah notices and values people the world might overlook.
1 THE year is 1473 B.C.E. The nation of Israel has just begun its conquest of the Promised Land. The Israelites have won extraordinary victories over the cities of Jericho and Ai. But later, something unexpected happens. A group of travelers comes to them, claiming to be from a distant land and seeking to establish a peace agreement with God's people.

2 Those travelers are the Gibeonites. This is the first time that the Gibeonites are mentioned in the Bible record, but it is not the last. A number of Bible accounts reveal that the Gibeonites were closely associated with the Israelites throughout the following centuries. These accounts contain valuable lessons for us, and they reveal much about Jehovah God.
3. (a) Who were the Gibeonites? (b) Why did they seek peace with Israel?

(a) The Gibeonites lived in the fortified city of Gibeon and were part of the Hivite nation — one of seven Canaanite nations that were, as Deut. 7:1 notes, "more populous and mightier" than Israel.

(b) Despite being militarily strong (Josh. 10:2), the Gibeonites could see that Jehovah was fighting for Israel and had promised to drive out the Canaanites (Ex. 34:11; Josh. 9:24). Rather than resist a battle they knew they'd lose, they sent a delegation to Joshua seeking peace.

💬 Comment you could share: What struck me was that the Gibeonites' strength didn't blind them to reality — they were militarily capable, yet humble enough to recognize Jehovah's hand and act accordingly.
📌 Ministry connection: People today, like the Gibeonites, often recognize evidence of a greater power at work even before they fully understand who Jehovah is — that recognition can be a starting point in a Bible discussion.
3 When the Israelites began their conquest of the land of Canaan, the Gibeonites lived in the fortified city of Gibeon. They were part of the Hivite nation, one of the seven Canaanite nations that were "more populous and mightier" than the Israelites. (Deut. 7:1) So the Gibeonites were a militarily strong people. (Josh. 10:2) Unlike the other Canaanites, however, they realized that resisting Israel would be pointless. They could see that Jehovah was fighting for his people, and they knew that he had promised to drive the Canaanites out of the land. (Ex. 34:11; Josh. 9:24) So after the Israelites conquered Jericho and Ai, the Gibeonites sent a delegation to Joshua at Gilgal in an effort to seek peace.
4. (a) As shown at Joshua 9:8-13, how did the Gibeonites trick Israel into making a covenant of peace? (b) What happened when their deception was discovered?

(a) Read Joshua 9:8-13 — The Gibeonites pretended to be from a distant land, carefully avoiding any mention of Jericho and Ai since that news couldn't plausibly have reached a "very distant land" yet. Without consulting Jehovah, Israel's representatives accepted their evidence and made a covenant of peace.

(b) When the deception was discovered shortly afterward, Israel still honored the covenant because they had "sworn an oath to them by Jehovah" (Josh. 9:16-19). The Gibeonites were assigned the humble role of "gatherers of wood and drawers of water for the assembly and for Jehovah's altar" (Josh. 9:27).

💬 Comment you could share: I found it interesting that Israel's leaders felt bound by an oath even though they'd been deceived into making it — that says something about how seriously Jehovah's people were meant to take their word.
🙏 Getting to know Jehovah: Even though the Israelites acted without consulting him, Jehovah didn't undermine the oath his people had sworn — he values integrity to one's word so highly that he let the consequences stand.
4 Read Joshua 9:8-13. The Gibeonites pretended to be from a distant land. They acknowledged that Jehovah had a hand in Israel's military success against Egypt and the Amorite Kings Sihon and Og. But wisely, they did not mention what had happened to Jericho and Ai, since such recent news could not yet have reached their so-called "very distant land." Without consulting Jehovah, Israel's representatives accepted the evidence that the Gibeonites presented and made a covenant of peace with them. (Josh. 9:14, 15) Shortly thereafter, the Gibeonites' deception was discovered, but the Israelites stuck to their peace agreement with the Gibeonites because they had "sworn an oath to them by Jehovah." (Josh. 9:16-19) The Gibeonites were given the humble assignment to serve indefinitely as "gatherers of wood and drawers of water for the assembly and for Jehovah's altar."—Josh. 9:27.
5. In what ways did the Gibeonites show faith in Jehovah?

When five Amorite kings banded together to attack Gibeon for making peace with Israel, the Gibeonites appealed to Joshua for help (Josh. 10:3-7). Joshua's rescue was blessed in a remarkable way — Jehovah hurled hailstones on the enemy and miraculously extended the daylight for battle (Josh. 10:9-14). By seeking peace and then appealing for help when threatened, the Gibeonites showed real faith in Jehovah's ability to fulfill his word and rescue them.

💬 Comment you could share: What struck me was that faith wasn't a one-time act for the Gibeonites — first they trusted Jehovah enough to seek peace, and later they trusted him enough to call for help when danger came.
5 On hearing that the Gibeonites had made peace with Israel, five Amorite kings banded together to attack Gibeon. The Gibeonites appealed to Joshua for help. (Josh. 10:3-7) Joshua led Israel in a rescue operation, and their efforts were blessed. Jehovah even hurled down great hailstones on the enemy Amorite forces and miraculously extended the daylight for battle. (Josh. 10:9-14) By seeking a covenant of peace with Israel and by appealing to Joshua for help when threatened, the Gibeonites showed faith in Jehovah—faith in his ability to fulfill his word and to rescue them.
6. What do we learn about Jehovah from the way he treated the Gibeonites during Israel's conquest of the Promised Land?

Jehovah is humble and merciful. He had instructed Israel to "drive away all the inhabitants of the land," which included the Gibeonites (Num. 33:51, 52). Yet in support of the covenant Israel had made, Jehovah mercifully permitted the Gibeonites to live — even though Israel hadn't consulted him first (Josh. 9:26; Josh. 11:19).

🙏 Getting to know Jehovah: Jehovah's willingness to work with an imperfect decision his people made — rather than simply overriding it — shows how much he values his people's integrity, even when they haven't consulted him as they should have.
6 What does this account teach us about Jehovah? Jehovah is humble and merciful. He had previously instructed Israel to "drive away all the inhabitants of the land," which included the Gibeonites. (Num. 33:51, 52) Yet, in support of the covenant of peace Israel made with them, Jehovah mercifully permitted the Gibeonites to live. Jehovah did so even though the Israelites failed to consult him before entering into that covenant. He then helped Israel to stick to their agreement and miraculously rescued the Gibeonites.—Josh. 9:26; 11:19.
7. How can we imitate the Gibeonites' faith and humility?

By cultivating strong faith in Jehovah — we know far more about him than the Gibeonites did, giving us even more reason to trust him fully (Ps. 40:4, 5). We can also imitate them by willingly doing humble tasks in Jehovah's service (Josh. 9:23, 27). The example of Luke, a young brother who watched a prominent Bethelite quietly volunteer for a night shift guarding a Kingdom Hall construction site, illustrates that "humility is not simply a feeling. It is expressed by what we choose to do or not do."

💬 Comment you could share: What struck me about Luke's account is that the older brother didn't need anyone to see him do that humble task — Luke just happened to notice, which makes the example even more genuine.
📌 Ministry connection: When we quietly take on unglamorous assignments in the congregation or the ministry, we're demonstrating the same spirit the Gibeonites showed — and that consistency speaks louder than words to those watching.
7 How can we imitate the Gibeonites? By cultivating strong faith in Jehovah. Since we know so much more about Jehovah than did the Gibeonites at that time, we have even more reasons to trust in Jehovah completely. (Ps. 40:4, 5) We can also imitate the Gibeonites by being willing to do humble tasks in Jehovah's service. (Josh. 9:23, 27) A young brother named Luke will never forget the time when he saw an older brother of the Bethel family do just that. Although that Bethelite cared for a weighty and somewhat prominent assignment, he quietly volunteered for a night shift to watch the local Kingdom Hall site while it was under construction. Luke says: "I learned that humility is not simply a feeling. It is expressed by what we choose to do or not do." May we express our faith in Jehovah daily by relying on him when we face difficult situations and by humbly caring for any theocratic assignment.
Wait on Jehovah to Correct Injustice
8. What crime did King Saul commit against the Gibeonites?

Centuries later, King Saul turned against the Gibeonites living in Israel. "In his zeal for the people of Israel and Judah," he attempted to wipe them out, and a number of them were murdered (2 Sam. 21:2, 5, 6). Saul broke the long-standing covenant of peace between Israel and the Gibeonites.

💬 Comment you could share: I found it sobering that misplaced "zeal" — Saul apparently thought he was doing something patriotic — led him to break a centuries-old promise and commit murder.
8 The Gibeonites are mentioned again in the Bible record centuries later. King Saul turned against the Gibeonites living in Israel. "In his zeal for the people of Israel and Judah," he attempted to wipe the Gibeonites out. As a result, a number of them were murdered. (2 Sam. 21:2, 5, 6) What an injustice! Saul broke the long-standing covenant of peace that existed between Israel and the Gibeonites.
9. When was the injustice against the Gibeonites finally addressed?

Not immediately. It wasn't until the reign of Saul's successor, King David, that Jehovah brought the matter to national attention by means of a three-year famine. When David inquired, Jehovah revealed that Saul's house was bloodguilty for the way they had mistreated the Gibeonites years earlier (2 Sam. 21:1).

🙏 Getting to know Jehovah: Jehovah's timing here — years passing before the injustice was addressed — shows he doesn't forget wrongs done to those he cares about, even when it seems the matter has gone unnoticed for a long time.
9 This terrible injustice against the Gibeonites was not immediately addressed. In fact, it was not until the reign of Saul's successor, King David, that Jehovah brought the matter to the attention of the nation by means of a three-year famine. When David asked about the reason for the famine, Jehovah told him that Saul and his household were bloodguilty because of the way they had mistreated the Gibeonites years earlier.—2 Sam. 21:1.
10. As recorded at 2 Samuel 21:3-6, how did the Gibeonites show respect for God's Law in what they said to David?

Read 2 Samuel 21:3-6 — David asked the Gibeonites what could atone for Saul's crime. Rather than demanding money, they rightly answered it was "not a matter of silver or gold," since the Law didn't permit a ransom for a murderer (Num. 35:30, 31). They also recognized they had no authority to execute anyone themselves. Ultimately David authorized the execution of seven of Saul's descendants, and the rains that followed signaled the matter was resolved to Jehovah's satisfaction (2 Sam. 21:9, 10, 14).

💬 Comment you could share: What struck me was that the Gibeonites, even as the wronged party, stayed within the boundaries of the Law rather than taking justice fully into their own hands — that's a level of restraint worth noting.
10 Read 2 Samuel 21:3-6. David spoke to the Gibeonites to try to determine what could be done to atone for Saul's crime against them. Would they try to get rich by making David pay for not acting earlier in their behalf? The Gibeonites rightly answered that it was "not a matter of silver or gold" because, according to the Law, no ransom could be accepted for a murderer. (Num. 35:30, 31) They also recognized that they could not put a man to death without legal authorization. In the end, David authorized the execution of seven of Saul's male descendants who may have had some share in Saul's attempted genocide. Sometime later, the rains came, ending the famine and signaling that the injustice had been avenged to Jehovah's satisfaction.—2 Sam. 21:9, 10, 14.
11. What can we learn about Jehovah from this account?

Jehovah is a just God who wants all people treated fairly — including those discriminated against, as is often the case with foreigners and minorities (Ps. 37:28). In his due time he corrects injustices his worshippers experience, and he expects us to keep our promises just as he expected Israel to keep theirs (compare Amos 1:9).

🙏 Getting to know Jehovah: The fact that Jehovah cared this much about the treatment of a non-Israelite minority group tells us something significant about how he views fairness across ethnic and national lines.
11 What does this account teach us about Jehovah? We see clear evidence that Jehovah is a just God. (Ps. 37:28) He wants all people to be treated fairly. That includes even those who may be discriminated against, as is often the case with foreigners and minorities. In his due time, Jehovah will correct any injustices that his worshippers may have experienced. We also learn that Jehovah expects us to keep our promises, just as he expected the Israelites to keep their promise of peace with the Gibeonites.—Compare Amos 1:9.
12. How can we imitate the Gibeonites when dealing with an injustice?

Even when mistreated by fellow believers, we can wait patiently on Jehovah, trusting he'll correct matters in his due time (Isa. 26:3, 4). Sister Laura French's example illustrates this: after being wrongly dismissed from Bethel over a false accusation, she never complained, spent four years pioneering zealously, and was eventually invited back — serving faithfully for roughly 50 more years.

💬 Comment you could share: Sister French's response really stood out to me — she didn't put her service on hold while waiting to be vindicated; she just kept serving faithfully in whatever way was open to her.
📌 Ministry connection: When people we're teaching face unfair treatment, her example is a practical, real-life illustration of patient trust in Jehovah rather than bitterness.
12 How can we imitate the Gibeonites? Even when mistreated by our brothers and sisters, we can wait patiently on Jehovah. By doing so, we show that we trust that Jehovah will correct matters in his due time. Consider the example of Sister Laura French. She began serving at Canada Bethel in 1926. About ten years later, she became the victim of a terrible injustice. She was wrongly accused of being involved with a group of apostates, and she was dismissed from Bethel. What would she do? Although she felt hurt, she never complained. She spent the next four years zealously serving as a pioneer. To her surprise and delight, in 1940 she was invited to return to Bethel, where she continued to serve faithfully until she finished her earthly course some 50 years later. Like Sister French, we can overcome an injustice by persevering in doing what is right and by waiting on Jehovah to correct matters in his due time.—Isa. 26:3, 4.
Loyally Support True Worship
13. Who were "the Nethinim," and what did they do after the Babylonian exile?

About 500 years after David, the 70-year Babylonian exile ended. In 537 B.C.E. exiles returned with Governor Zerubbabel (Ezra 2:1, 2, 58), and in 468 B.C.E. another group returned with Ezra (Ezra 7:1-7). Both groups included families of "the Nethinim" — non-Israelite temple servants, many likely descendants of the Gibeonites.

💬 Comment you could share: It's remarkable that descendants of the Gibeonites were still faithfully serving at the temple roughly a thousand years after the original covenant with Joshua — that's incredible continuity.
13 About 500 years after the time of King David, the Gibeonites appear again in the Bible record. The 70-year Babylonian exile had come to an end, and in 537 B.C.E., the first group of Jewish exiles returned to Jerusalem with Governor Zerubbabel. (Ezra 2:1, 2, 58) In 468 B.C.E., another group returned but with Ezra the copyist. (Ezra 7:1-7) Both of those relatively small groups of returning exiles included families of "the Nethinim." Who were the Nethinim? They were "non-Israelite temple servants, or ministers," and many of them were likely descendants of the Gibeonites.—See Glossary, "Nethinim."
14. How did the Gibeonites show loyalty to Jehovah? (1 Chronicles 9:2 and footnote)

Read 1 Chronicles 9:2 and footnote — "The temple servants" were among the very first exiles to return, even though many other Jews found prosperity in Babylon and were unwilling to make the sacrifices needed to rebuild. Faithful exiles, including likely descendants of the Gibeonites, longed to see true worship reestablished and made every effort to return — even though, unlike the Jews, they had no land inheritance in Israel to reclaim.

💬 Comment you could share: What struck me is that the Gibeonites' descendants had nothing material to gain from returning — no land inheritance waiting for them — yet they went anyway, purely to support true worship.
📌 Ministry connection: This is a strong illustration for discussing what motivates genuine devotion — not personal gain, but love for true worship itself.
14 Read 1 Chronicles 9:2 and footnote. "The temple servants" were among the first exiles to return to Israel. That is significant because not all of the Jews in exile returned. Many Jews no doubt had found a measure of prosperity in Babylon, and they were unwilling to make the sacrifices required to return and help rebuild their devastated homeland. But faithful exiles, including some who were likely descendants of the Gibeonites, longed to see true worship reestablished at the temple in Jerusalem, and they made every effort to return. Unlike the Jews, the descendants of the Gibeonites did not have an inheritance of land in Israel. Yet, they returned to care for their temple duties and even to help repair Jerusalem's walls.—Neh. 3:26.
15. What does the account of the Gibeonites teach us about Jehovah?

Jehovah greatly appreciates his loyal worshippers and always cares for them. By the time the exile ended, almost 1,000 years had passed since Jehovah saved the Gibeonites during the conquest — yet he continued blessing them. Their descendants survived Jerusalem's destruction and continued ministering with the Levites at the temple, some living near it (Ezra 2:70; Neh. 11:21), and received an exemption from tax, tribute, and toll for their temple work (Ezra 7:24).

🙏 Getting to know Jehovah: Jehovah's care for the Gibeonites across roughly a millennium shows his loyalty isn't short-term — it spans generations for those who remain devoted to him.
15 What does this account teach us about Jehovah? We learn that our God greatly appreciates his loyal worshippers and always takes care of them. By the time the Babylonian exile ended, almost 1,000 years had passed since Jehovah saved the Gibeonites during Israel's conquest of the Promised Land. Yet, he continued to bless them. Descendants of the Gibeonites survived the destruction of Judah and Jerusalem in 607 B.C.E. After the exile, they continued to be closely involved with true worship, ministering with the Levites at the temple. Some apparently lived near the temple. (Ezra 2:70; Neh. 11:21) And they received an exemption from paying tax, tribute, and toll on account of their temple work.—Ezra 7:24.
16. How can we imitate the Gibeonites when it comes to our worship?

We want to make sacrifices to serve Jehovah because we love him — like Alwin, a brother in the Philippines with a high-paying oil-company job who kept taking time off to support circuit overseer visits. Eventually he quit his job to become a regular pioneer, and with his wife has helped 21 people learn the truth (Matt. 6:33).

💬 Comment you could share: What I appreciated about Alwin's account is that the decision wasn't sudden — he tested the waters first by taking time off for the circuit overseer's visits before making the bigger leap into pioneering.
📌 Ministry connection: Alwin and his wife's fruitage — 21 people helped to learn the truth — is a concrete, encouraging example to share when discussing the rewards of prioritizing Kingdom interests.
16 How can we imitate the Gibeonites? We too want to do all we can to support true worship. We willingly make sacrifices to serve Jehovah because we love him and want to please him. That was true of Alwin, a brother in the Philippines. He had a high-paying job working for an oil company. But he wanted to do more in his service to Jehovah. Whenever the circuit overseer visited his congregation, Alwin took time off from work to support the visit and enjoyed having a greater share in the ministry. After some time and many prayers, Alwin decided to quit his job and find one that would allow him to serve as a regular pioneer. Doing so would involve making financial sacrifices. Still, he reached his goal. His wife joined him in the pioneer ministry, and as a couple they have been able to help 21 people learn the truth. No doubt, Jehovah values this couple's efforts. We too can be confident that Jehovah appreciates any sacrifices we make in his service, and as a result, he will always care for our needs.—Matt. 6:33.
17. What have we learned by reviewing Bible accounts about the Gibeonites?

The Bible accounts about the Gibeonites shed light on Jehovah's endearing personality — humble, merciful, just, and loyal, and he always rewards his faithful worshippers. Like the Gibeonites, we want strong faith in Jehovah, humility to care for any theocratic assignment, patience to wait on Jehovah regarding injustices, and zeal and loyalty in supporting true worship.

17 The Bible accounts about the Gibeonites shed light on Jehovah's endearing personality. He is humble, merciful, just, and loyal. He always rewards his faithful worshippers. These Bible accounts also contain a number of practical lessons. Like the Gibeonites, we want to have strong faith in Jehovah, relying on him when faced with frightening events. Such faith will also move us to care humbly for any theocratic assignment we may receive. We want to wait patiently on Jehovah, confident that he will ultimately correct all injustices. And we support true worship with zeal and loyalty. As we do, we will show that we have learned valuable lessons from the Gibeonites.

We imitate them by trusting Jehovah fully — we know even more about him than they did — and by being willing to do humble tasks in his service.
