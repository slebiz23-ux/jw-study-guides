⛏️ TREASURES FROM GOD'S WORD

1. What Made "Figs" Good or Bad? (10 min.)

▶
Two baskets of figs in front of Jehovah's temple — one good, one rotten (Jeremiah 24).
This vivid picture sets up the whole chapter: Jehovah used something as ordinary as figs to teach a lesson about the heart condition of his people. It's a reminder that appearances (being "in Jerusalem" vs. exiled) don't determine Jehovah's view of us — our heart attitude does.

Repentant exiled Jews were like good figs — Jer 24:1, 2 5-7

"This is what Jehovah the God of Israel says, 'Like these good figs, so I will regard in a good way the exiles of Judah... I will give them a heart to know me, that I am Jehovah. They will become my people, and I will become their God, for they will return to me with all their heart.'" — Jeremiah 24:5-7 (NWT Study Edition)

Disobedient, unrepentant Jews were like bad figs — Jer 24:8-10

"But concerning the bad figs that are so bad they cannot be eaten... this is what Jehovah says: 'So I will regard King Zedekiah of Judah, his princes, the remnant of Jerusalem who are left in this land... I will make them an object of horror and calamity to all the kingdoms of the earth, a reproach, a proverbial saying, a cause for ridicule, and a curse.'" — Jeremiah 24:8, 9 (NWT Study Edition)

Christians can be like good figs by doing an honest self-examination and by making needed adjustments.

💬 Comment you could share:
I found it interesting that Jehovah didn't judge the exiles by their circumstances — being taken from Jerusalem could have looked like punishment, yet he called them "good figs" because of their heart. It shows that our location or situation in life isn't what matters most to Jehovah.
📌 Personal application:
When I go through a hard change — a move, a job loss, a health setback — this account reminds me to ask "what is Jehovah doing with my heart through this?" instead of just wishing for the old circumstances back.
🙏 Getting to know Jehovah:
This account shows Jehovah as a God who looks past outward appearances and evaluates the heart — and who is willing to "build up" and "plant" those who turn back to him with their whole heart.

2. Spiritual Gems (10 min.)

▶
Jer 25:8, 9, 12 — Why did Jehovah punish Babylon when it acted as his "servant" to execute judgment on his people?

Jer 25:8, 9, 12

"Therefore this is what Jehovah of armies says, '"Because you would not obey my words, I am sending for all the families of the north," declares Jehovah, "sending for King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon, my servant..."'" — Jeremiah 25:8, 9 (NWT Study Edition)
"'But when 70 years have been fulfilled, I will call to account the king of Babylon and that nation for their error,' declares Jehovah, 'and I will make the land of the Chaldeans a desolate wasteland for all time.'" — Jeremiah 25:12 (NWT Study Edition)
Jehovah used Babylon as an instrument of judgment, but Babylon acted out of its own ambition and cruelty, not obedience to Jehovah — so once it had served its purpose, Jehovah held it accountable for its own errors and excessive brutality. (See Insight on the Scriptures, "Kingdom of God" ¶36.)
💬 Comment you could share:
It struck me that Jehovah can use even a wicked nation to accomplish his purpose, yet he still holds that nation responsible for how it acted. Being "used" by Jehovah's providence never excuses cruelty or pride.
📌 Personal application:
This helps me trust that even when unfair things happen because of others' bad choices, Jehovah is still keeping accounts — nothing done against his people goes unnoticed forever.
What spiritual gems from this week's Bible reading would you like to share?
One gem worth sharing: in Jer 24:8-10 Jehovah names Zedekiah and "the remnant of Jerusalem" as the bad figs — proof that living in Jehovah's favored city didn't automatically make someone approved. It's a good gem on not relying on outward religious status.

8. "Sarah — She Refused to Look Back" (30 min.) wcg chap. 3

▶
Sarah and Abraham leaving the comforts of Ur, traveling by camel with their household.
Sarah walked away from a walled city with running water, a marketplace, and security — for a tent. It's a strong reminder that faith sometimes means giving up comfort, not just risk.
A piece of jewelry unearthed at the site of ancient Ur (photo courtesy of the British Museum).
This artifact helps me appreciate just how materially rich Ur was — Sarah left behind real luxury, not a hardship post, showing her move was a matter of faith, not necessity.

Sarah's submissiveness to Abraham didn't mean she stayed silent — she spoke up plainly when something mattered. That's a healthy balance worth applying in my own close relationships.

This is the modern parallel to Sarah's example: trading comfort and familiarity for a simpler life in order to put Kingdom interests first.

Bible account: Genesis 12:1-20; 15:18; 16:1, 2; 17:3-8, 15-19; 18:9-15; 20:1-17; 21:1-13   Acts 7:1-7

Printed Questions

1. Was it wrong for Sarah to marry Abraham even though he was her half brother? ▶
No — in Sarah and Abraham's day, before the Mosaic Law was given, marriage between close relatives was not prohibited by God and was a common, culturally accepted practice for preserving family lines and property. (Reference: wp17.3 12 ¶5, footnote)
2. Describe what Sarah may have left behind in Ur. ▶
Ur was a prosperous, walled city on the Euphrates with solid houses, courtyards, running water, guarded security, and an active marketplace supplied by river trade. Archaeological finds — like fine jewelry uncovered there — show that some residents enjoyed real wealth and refinement, which Sarah gave up to live in tents. (Reference: wp17.3 13 ¶3–14 ¶2)
3. What evidence shows that Abraham acted wisely when he concealed Sarah's identity as his wife? ▶
Twice — with Pharaoh in Egypt and later with Abimelech — presenting Sarah as his "sister" (a half-truth, since she was also his half sister) protected Abraham's life in cultures where a ruler might kill a husband to take a beautiful wife. In both cases, Jehovah personally intervened to rescue Sarah and return her unharmed, showing that Abraham's caution didn't rely on the deception itself but on Jehovah's protection. (Reference: wp17.3 14 ¶6–15 ¶2)
4. Although Sarah was submissive to Abraham, does that mean she never spoke up? Explain. ▶
No. Sarah's submissiveness was not passivity — she spoke frankly to Abraham about the tension between Hagar/Ishmael and Isaac, and the Bible record shows Jehovah himself told Abraham to listen to what Sarah said. Genuine submission can include honest, respectful communication. (Reference: g 1/08 29 ¶4-5)

Reflect on the Lessons

Compare Sarah and Lot's wife — in what ways were they similar? In what ways were they different?
Both women left settled homes to follow their husbands' course of faith. But Lot's wife longed for what was left behind and looked back, losing her life, while Sarah — per Hebrews 11:15 — refused to keep remembering the place she had departed from.
"And yet, if they had kept remembering the place from which they had departed, they would have had opportunity to return." — Hebrews 11:15 (NWT Study Edition)
Sarah was very beautiful and her husband wealthy — what example does she set regarding our view of physical or material advantages?
She never leaned on those advantages for security or let them define her worth; her lasting praise came from fearing Jehovah, not from beauty or wealth. Prov 31:30
"Charm may be false, and beauty may be fleeting, but the woman who fears Jehovah will be praised." — Proverbs 31:30 (NWT Study Edition)
💬 Comment you could share:
I never thought about how much Sarah gave up materially just to move to Canaan — it wasn't hardship that tested her faith at first, it was giving up comfort.
📌 Personal application:
When I'm tempted to romanticize "how things used to be" before a change I made for spiritual reasons, I want to imitate Sarah and refuse to look back.

Wrap-Up Questions

What does this teach us about Jehovah?
Jehovah rewards patient faith even when the promise is delayed for decades, and he personally intervenes to protect those who trust him, as he did for Sarah in Egypt and with Abimelech. He also cares about the details of an imperfect woman's private struggle — her inability to have a child — and acted at just the right time.
"Jehovah turned his attention to Sarah just as he had said, and Jehovah did for Sarah what he had promised. So Sarah became pregnant and then bore a son to Abraham in his old age at the appointed time God had promised him." — Genesis 21:1, 2 (NWT Study Edition)
Are there any points from this lesson we can use in the ministry or our daily life?
Sarah's willingness to leave material comfort behind is a strong illustration for people today who feel they have "too much to lose" by making changes for Bible-based reasons — in the ministry, or in my own choices about how I use time and resources.
