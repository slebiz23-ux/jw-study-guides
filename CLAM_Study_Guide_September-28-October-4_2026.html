1. Continue Supporting One Another

10 min. — Talk · Jeremiah 38–39

Jerusalem is under Babylonian siege. The princes have thrown Jeremiah into a muddy cistern to die, and weak King Zedekiah has let them. Into that moment steps Ebed-melech, a court official who risks his own safety to speak up. The talk draws three ways we can support one another from the account: speak up for others, comfort others, and encourage others to obey Jehovah.

Ebed-melech kneeling before King Zedekiah and pleading for Jeremiah’s life as royal guards watch.
🖼️ What the picture teachesNotice the setting: armed guards, a king who had just said he “cannot do anything to stop” the princes, and Ebed-melech on his knees in public. Speaking up was not safe or convenient. Yet he did it respectfully (“O my lord the king”) and plainly (“what these men have done … is evil!”). Supporting a brother or sister sometimes means being the one person willing to say something — tactfully, but clearly.

Insight adds that “eunuch” here likely means a court officer; he evidently had authority, since the king put 30 men under his command. And the 2012 article highlights his kindness too: the rags and cloths (Jer 38:11, 12) were so the ropes would not tear into Jeremiah’s armpits.

He passed on Jehovah’s comforting promise. A true friend in distress uses whatever he has — even if that is only words from Jehovah.

Encourage others to obey Jehovah as Jeremiah did
Jer. 38:20

Even to the king who had allowed him to be thrown into a pit, Jeremiah said “please.” He didn’t lecture or retaliate; he kindly urged obedience and pointed to the outcome — “it will go well with you.”

Passage summary & reading tips ▶

Summary: Four princes hear Jeremiah’s message urging surrender (vv. 1-3) and demand his death, calling him a demoralizer (v. 4). Zedekiah hands him over (v. 5), and Jeremiah is lowered into a muddy cistern (v. 6). Ebed-melech hears, confronts the king at the Gate of Benjamin (vv. 7-9), and is sent with 30 men (v. 10). He gently lowers rags so Jeremiah can be pulled out safely (vv. 11-13).

Applying “Warmth and Empathy” (th 12):

  • Think about your listeners — many in the audience have been unjustly treated or felt abandoned; read verse 6 knowing that.
  • Express the feelings in the passage without overacting: the princes’ hostility in verse 4, Zedekiah’s weak resignation in verse 5, and the urgency and warmth of Ebed-melech’s plea in verse 9.
  • Warm the tone by drawing out vowels rather than clipping consonants — especially in verse 12, where Ebed-melech speaks kindly to Jeremiah.
  • Practice names in advance: Sheph·a·tiʹah, Ged·a·liʹah, Juʹcal, Shel·e·miʹah, Pashʹhur, Mal·chiʹjah, Eʹbed-melʹech, E·thi·oʹpi·an.

8. wcg chap. 10 — Moses: “He Made His Choice”

30 min. — Walk Courageously With God, chapter 10
① Narrative summary & Bible account ▶

Named by the Egyptian princess who saved him from the water, Moses was raised in Pharaoh’s household and “instructed in all the wisdom of the Egyptians” — “powerful in his words and deeds.” He could have had wealth, prominence, and pleasure. But at 40 he made his choice clear: he refused to be called the son of Pharaoh’s daughter and chose to suffer with God’s people.

His choice was right, but his way of acting on it was not. Seeing an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, he “looked this way and that,” killed the Egyptian, and hid the body. The next day a Hebrew in the wrong threw it back at him: “Who appointed you as a prince and a judge over us?” Moses thought his brothers would understand that God was using him to save them — they didn’t. He had misread Jehovah’s timing and his own people, and he still lacked a quality Jehovah values highly: meekness.

Pharaoh sought to kill him, so Moses fled — leaving wealth and comfort behind — to Midian, where he would spend decades as “a foreign resident in a foreign land.” Would he have the courage to grow and change?

③ Dig Deeper (Q&A) ▶

1. What historical evidence is consistent with the Bible’s account that the Israelites were slaves in Egypt? w20.03 30

Picture A: Part of an ancient Egyptian list of slaves that features more than 40 Semitic names. — Courtesy Brooklyn Museum, gift of Theodora Wilbour

Answer: Archaeologists have found evidence of 20 or more Semitic settlements in northern Egypt from the period that fits the patriarchs. From southern Egypt, a papyrus from the Middle Kingdom lists slaves of a single household, and more than 40 of the names are Semitic — some resembling Bible names such as Issachar, Asher, and Shiphrah (Ex 1:3, 4, 15). Scholars conclude that the Bible’s account of bondage in Egypt has a firm historical basis.

🖼️ ApplicationA household slave list with Hebrew-sounding names reminds me that Exodus is real history, not legend. Details like these build my confidence to defend the Bible in the ministry.

2. How might Moses have been taught by his parents and then “instructed in all the wisdom of the Egyptians”? Acts 7:22 w02 6/15 10 ¶2-5

So Moses was instructed in all the wisdom of the Egyptians. In fact, he was powerful in his words and deeds.— Acts 7:22 (NWTsty)

Answer: Moses “grew up” with Amram and Jochebed — at least until weaned and possibly longer — and they no doubt used that time to teach him his Hebrew origin and about Jehovah. Back with Pharaoh’s daughter, he received training fit for government office: mathematics, geometry, architecture, construction, other sciences, and likely Egyptian religion. He may have been schooled alongside royal children and foreign princes in palace “nurseries.” Court life tested him with wealth, luxury, power, and moral danger — the question was where his loyalty would lie.

📌 Personal application:Moses’ parents had limited time, but what they taught him held. Parents today can make the most of every early year — and trust that faith planted in a child’s heart can outlast years of worldly influence.

3. How did the Egyptians usually bury their dead? How might they have felt when Moses not only killed an Egyptian but also hid the body instead of embalming and burying it? it “Egypt, Egyptian” ¶28

Picture B: An Egyptian mummy on display at a museum.

Answer: Egyptians believed the soul was immortal but needed the body preserved so it could return and use it, so they embalmed their dead and placed them in tombs — the deceased’s “home” — stocked with jewelry, clothing, furniture, food, and spells. Hiding a murdered Egyptian in the sand, unembalmed, would have been seen not only as murder but as robbing him of his afterlife — an outrage that helps explain why Pharaoh wanted Moses dead.

🖼️ ApplicationThe mummy shows how deeply false beliefs about death shaped Egyptian life. Knowing the truth about the dead (Eccl 9:5) frees us from such fears — a truth worth sharing.

4. Moses fled to Midian. What did the Midianites and the Israelites have in common? it “Midian” No. 2 ¶1-2

Answer: The Midianites descended from Midian, Abraham’s son by Keturah — so they were relatives of the Israelites through Abraham. They likely spoke a language closely resembling Hebrew (Gideon apparently understood them without difficulty). Moses fled, not to a completely foreign culture, but to distant relatives of his own people.

🙏 Getting to know Jehovah:Even in exile, Jehovah led Moses to a place and family where he could adapt — and to a priest who welcomed him into his home (Ex 2:20, 21). Jehovah prepares a place for us even when we have to start over.

What can Moses’ example teach you about controlling your temper?

So he looked this way and that, and seeing no one, he killed the Egyptian and hid him in the sand.— Ex. 2:12 (NWTsty)
💬 Comment you could share:“The chapter says we don’t know if Moses meant to kill the man or if anger made him strike harder than he intended. Either way, one moment of uncontrolled anger cost him 40 years in exile. That reminds me that good motives don’t excuse a bad temper — I need meekness, not just zeal.”

What can youths learn from Moses about courageously choosing the right goals?

Picture C: A brother in high school carefully considering his future — a prestigious job designing airplanes, or using the “Enjoy Life Forever!” brochure to study the Bible with a man.
🖼️ ApplicationLike Moses, this young brother has real ability and attractive options. Moses “considered the reproach of the Christ to be riches greater than the treasures of Egypt” because “he looked intently toward the payment of the reward” (Heb 11:26). Youths can use their talents without letting a career become the treasure they chase.
💬 Comment you could share:“Moses had the best education and prospects in the world, and he gave them up. What helped him was looking ahead to the reward. A young person today can ask, ‘Twenty years from now, which choice will I be glad I made?’”

How else might you imitate the courage shown by Moses in this account?

But as usual, the shepherds came and drove them away. At this Moses got up and helped the women and watered their flock.— Ex. 2:17 (NWTsty)
📌 Personal application:Even as a fugitive, tired and alone, Moses stood up for the women being bullied at the well. I can show that kind of courage by helping someone who’s being treated unfairly, even when I’m dealing with my own problems.
⑤ Meditate on the Bigger Picture — wrap-up questions ▶

What does this teach us about Jehovah?

🙏 Getting to know Jehovah:Jehovah had a purpose for Moses, but he works on his own timetable — not ours. He didn’t discard Moses after a serious mistake; he patiently trained him for 40 years until he became the meekest man on earth (Num 12:3). And Jehovah is “the rewarder of those earnestly seeking him” (Heb 11:6), which is exactly what Moses was looking toward (Heb 11:26).
Moreover, without faith it is impossible to please God well, for whoever approaches God must believe that he is and that he becomes the rewarder of those earnestly seeking him.— Heb. 11:6 (NWTsty)

How does the account tie in with Jehovah’s purposes? Moses would become the one Jehovah used to deliver Israel, give the Law, and foreshadow the Christ. Hebrews even calls Moses’ sacrifice “the reproach of the Christ.” His early choices and training were part of preparing the nation from which the Messiah would come.
