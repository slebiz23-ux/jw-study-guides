1. Jehovah Rewards Faithful Obedience

Obedience to Jehovah has to be more than convenient — the account of the Rechabites shows what happens when obedience is genuine and enduring versus when it's given only under pressure.

Many Jews obeyed Jehovah only when they needed his help Jer 34:8-11 (jr 49-50 ¶13-14)

"The word that came to Jeremiah from Jehovah after King Zed·e·kiʹah had made a covenant with all the people in Jerusalem to proclaim liberty to them, that everyone should free his Hebrew slaves... So all the princes and all the people obeyed... However, they later brought back the male and female slaves whom they had freed, and they again forced them back into slavery." (Jer. 34:8-11, in part)

The Rechabites adhered closely to Jehonadab's command Jer 35:5, 6 (jr 76 ¶17-18)

Jehovah rewarded the Rechabites for their faithful obedience Jer 35:18, 19 (it "Rechabites" ¶5-6)

The Rechabites refuse Jeremiah's wine, staying true to Jehonadab's centuries-old command.
🖼️ What the picture shows: The contrast is striking — Jeremiah, a prophet of Jehovah, extends wine to the Rechabites right inside the temple, yet they still refuse out of loyalty to a mere human ancestor's command. It's a vivid, almost uncomfortable, picture of consistency under direct social pressure.
📖 Full sample talk outline

Opening (30 sec): Ask the audience: "Have you ever made a promise that got harder to keep the longer you kept it?" (← th study 3: open with a relatable question to draw in the audience)

Body point 1: Contrast Judah's on-and-off obedience (Jer 34:8-11) with the Rechabites' multi-generational consistency (Jer 35:5-10). (← th study 8: use contrast to make the point memorable)

Body point 2: Explain that Jehovah noticed and rewarded steady obedience even though the Rechabites weren't Israelites by birth (Jer 35:18-19). (← th study 6: draw a clear conclusion from the scriptures cited)

Application: Ask, "Am I resolved to obey Jehovah, even if at times I feel I could avoid the consequences of disobedience?" Pause briefly for silent reflection. (← th study 17: use box questions to prompt personal reflection, not just information)

💬 Comment you could share: "What struck me is that the Rechabites weren't even Israelites, yet Jehovah rewarded them for something the whole nation of Judah failed to do consistently — obey."
🙏 Getting to know Jehovah: He notices obedience even from unexpected people, and he doesn't overlook faithfulness just because someone lacks a long spiritual pedigree.

Jer 34:7 — What archaeological evidence supports the events described in this verse? Jer 34:7 (it "Archaeology" ¶27-28)

💬 Comment you could share: "This verse names Lachish and Azekah as the last fortified cities holding out against Babylon. Archaeologists actually found the 'Lachish Letters' — ostraca written right around this time — and one of them mentions that the signal fires of Azekah could no longer be seen, matching this verse almost exactly."

What spiritual gems from this week's Bible reading would you like to share?

Pick whichever chapter you were assigned to read — options are grouped below so you can choose one from the chapter you covered.

Chapter 34 — pick one

Jer. 34:5 Read

Even facing judgment for unfaithfulness, Zedekiah is told he'll die "in peace," not by the sword — a reminder that Jehovah's justice always leaves room for mercy where it's deserved.

Jer. 34:15, 16 Read

The people did what was right by freeing their slaves, then reversed course and "profaned my name." It's easy to overlook how quickly a good start can be undone — worth reflecting on whether I follow through on resolutions made to Jehovah.

Jer. 34:18, 19 Read

The covenant ceremony of cutting a calf in two and walking between the halves is a striking ancient custom I might otherwise skim past — it graphically pictured the fate of anyone who broke the covenant.

Chapter 35 — pick one

Jer. 35:6-10 Read

The Rechabites' lifestyle — no wine, no houses, no farming, living in tents for generations — is easy to read past quickly, but it shows a family so committed to their forefather's instruction that it shaped their whole way of life.

Jer. 35:14 Read

Jehovah points out that the Rechabites obeyed a human ancestor perfectly, "but you have not obeyed me" — a pointed, almost uncomfortable comparison that still applies today.

Jer. 35:19 Read

Jehovah promises the Rechabites will "never fail" to have a descendant serving him — a specific, personal reward for a family most of Judah probably looked down on.

CBS30 min. · Walk Courageously With God, chap. 8

10. Joseph — "He Resisted Temptation"

Joseph passes Potiphar's wife without engaging — courage shown in a small, repeated daily choice, not just a single dramatic moment.
🖼️ What the picture shows: Joseph isn't shown confronting her — he's shown simply walking past, focused on his task. It's a reminder that resisting temptation is often about consistently not engaging, rather than winning a dramatic argument every time.

Narrative summary: Joseph, favored by his father and hated by his brothers, was sold into slavery in Egypt. Jehovah blessed his diligent service to Potiphar, but Potiphar's wife repeatedly pressured him to sleep with her. Joseph refused — not just out of loyalty to his master, but because he refused to "sin against God." Falsely accused, he was imprisoned for years yet kept serving faithfully. Jehovah eventually used him to interpret Pharaoh's dreams, and Joseph was elevated to prime minister of Egypt, later saving his own family — the brothers who had betrayed him — from famine.

Read the Bible account:
Genesis 37:1-36 Genesis 39:1-23 Genesis 41:1–45:28

For discussion: In what ways did Joseph show courage?

💬 Comment you could share: "Joseph showed courage in ordinary, repeated moments — refusing Potiphar's wife 'day after day,' not just once — and in extraordinary ones, like forgiving the brothers who sold him into slavery."
🔎 Dig Deeper — 4 questions

1. What supports the accuracy of this account about Joseph's life in Egypt? (g 11/10 15 ¶2)

Answer: The Egyptian customs, titles, and details recorded in the account — such as embalming practices and Egyptian court protocol — match what archaeology and Egyptology have independently confirmed about that historical period, lending credibility to the record as a firsthand or well-informed account.

2. How did Joseph know that Jehovah would consider adultery an act of "great badness"? Gen. 39:9 (w22.08 26 ¶2)

"There is no one greater in this house than I am, and he has not withheld from me anything at all except you, because you are his wife. So how could I commit this great badness and actually sin against God?" (Gen. 39:9)
Answer: Joseph directly calls it sin against God, not merely a betrayal of Potiphar. This shows he already understood Jehovah's view of marriage and sexual purity as a moral standard from God himself, not just a social or contractual obligation to his master.

3. Why did Jehovah allow Joseph to become prime minister of Egypt? A (w96 5/1 11 ¶4)

"...because God has sent me ahead of you for the preservation of life." (Gen. 45:5)
Answer: Joseph's own explanation to his brothers was that his position let him "preserve life" — not just for Egypt, but for his own family and, in a larger sense, the developing nation of Israel through which the promised Seed would come.

4. What indicates that Joseph's faith never wavered, even at 110 years of age? Gen. 50:25, 26 B (w07 6/1 28 ¶10-11)

"So Joseph made the sons of Israel swear, saying: 'God will without fail turn his attention to you. You must take my bones up out of here.' And Joseph died at the age of 110, and they had him embalmed, and he was put in a coffin in Egypt." (Gen. 50:25, 26)
Answer: Even as an old man who had lived his whole adult life in Egyptian comfort and status, Joseph still believed Jehovah's promise to bring Israel out of Egypt into the Promised Land — so firmly that he made his family swear an oath about his own burial, decades before the exodus actually happened.
Picture A — Joseph, now prime minister of Egypt, directs the storage and distribution of grain during the years of plenty.
🖼️ Application: Joseph's rise wasn't a reward he sought — it came from consistently doing his best wherever Jehovah placed him, in slavery and in prison alike, long before any position of power was in view.
Picture B — Generations later, the Israelites carry Joseph's bones to the Promised Land, fulfilling his dying request.
🖼️ Application: Joseph never saw this day, yet he acted on faith in a promise he'd only see fulfilled long after his death — a picture of confidence in Jehovah's word outlasting one's own lifetime.
💭 Reflect on the Lessons

What do Joseph's dealings with his brothers teach you about how to react when you are mistreated by a fellow Christian? Gen. 45:4, 5 Gen. 50:19-21

"So Joseph said to his brothers: 'Come close to me, please.' ... 'I am Joseph your brother, whom you sold into Egypt. But now do not be upset and do not reproach one another because you sold me here; because God has sent me ahead of you for the preservation of life.'" (Gen. 45:4, 5)
"Joseph said to them: 'Do not be afraid. Am I in the place of God? Although you meant to harm me, God intended it to turn out well... So now do not be afraid. I will keep supplying you and your little children with food.' Thus he comforted them and spoke reassuringly to them." (Gen. 50:19-21)
💬 Comment you could share: "Joseph didn't just tolerate his brothers — he actively comforted and reassured them, twice, years apart. He also refused to put himself 'in the place of God' as their judge, leaving that to Jehovah."
