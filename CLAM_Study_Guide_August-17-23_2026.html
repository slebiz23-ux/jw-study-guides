⛏️ TREASURES FROM GOD'S WORD

This talk shows how Jehovah's people avoid being misled by false claims and stay anchored to genuine direction from Jehovah's organization — just as Jehovah's prophet Jeremiah spoke lifesaving truth while false prophets contradicted him.

Key scripture points

God's prophet Jeremiah gave lifesaving direction Jer 26:2-6

“2 “This is what Jehovah says, ‘Stand in the courtyard of the house of Jehovah and speak concerning all the people of the cities of Judah who are coming in to worship at the house of Jehovah. Tell them everything that I command you; do not take away a word. 3 Perhaps they will listen and each one will turn back from his evil way, and I will change my mind concerning the calamity that I intend to bring on them because of their evil deeds. 4 Say to them: “This is what Jehovah says, ‘If you will not listen to me by following my law that I have placed before you, 5 by listening to the words of my servants the prophets, whom I am sending to you again and again, whom you have not listened to, 6 then I will make this house like Shiʹloh, and I will make this city an object of cursing to all the nations of the earth.’”’””

Jer 26:14

“14 But as for me, I am in your hand. Do to me whatever seems good and right in your eyes.”

Jeremiah's calm answer — “do to me whatever seems good” — shows he left his life in Jehovah's hands rather than begging for mercy. I could mention how this models trust when facing pressure for standing up for truth.

Jer 26:16

“16 Then the princes and all the people said to the priests and the prophets: “This man does not deserve the death penalty, for he spoke to us in the name of Jehovah our God.””

It's easy to overlook that the princes and people actually changed their minds once they heard reasoned, Scripture-based argument (v.17-19). That shows the value of calmly reasoning from the Scriptures rather than reacting emotionally.

Jer 26:24

“24 But A·hiʹkam the son of Shaʹphan supported Jeremiah, so that he was not handed over to the people to be put to death.”

Ahikam isn't a headline name, but his quiet support kept Jeremiah alive. It's a good reminder that standing behind someone who is doing what's right — even without fanfare — really matters to Jehovah.

Chapter 27 — pick one

Jer 27:5, 6

“5 ‘It is I who made the earth, mankind, and the beasts that are on the surface of the earth by my great power and by my outstretched arm; and I have given it to whomever I please. 6 And now I have given all these lands into the hand of my servant King Neb·u·chad·nezʹzar of Babylon; even the wild beasts of the field I have given him to serve him.”

Jehovah describes himself as the one who made the earth and gives it to whomever he pleases — even allowing pagan Babylon temporary rule. This is a striking reminder of Jehovah's absolute sovereignty over world events, even when they seem to favor the wicked.

The nations that submitted to Babylon's yoke were allowed to “remain on its land.” I could compare this to how submitting to Jehovah's arrangements today, even when we don't love every detail, brings blessing and protection.

Jer 27:17

“17 Do not listen to them. Serve the king of Babylon and you will keep living. Why should this city become a ruin?”

Jeremiah's plain, practical appeal — “why should this city become a ruin?” — shows he reasoned with people using logic they could relate to, not just religious language.

Jer 28:9

“9 If a prophet prophesies about peace and the word of that prophet comes true, then it will be known that Jehovah truly sent that prophet.””

This verse gives a simple test for a true prophet: does the message actually come true? It's a good gem to connect with how we can have confidence in Jehovah's own track record of fulfilled prophecy.

Jer 28:16, 17

“16 Therefore this is what Jehovah says, ‘Look! I am removing you from the face of the ground. This year you will die, for you have urged rebellion against Jehovah.’” 17 So Han·a·niʹah the prophet died in that year, in the seventh month.”

Hananiah's popular, comforting message felt good to hear, but it was false, and the consequence was swift. It's sobering to consider how appealing a lie can sound compared to an unpopular truth.

1. Narrative

Jehovah promised Abraham a great nation and a homeland, but Satan targeted that family from the start. When an alliance of four eastern kings raided Sodom and carried off Abraham's nephew Lot, Abraham had every reason to stay out of it — he was a shepherd, not a warrior, vastly outnumbered, and risking his own safety and reputation. Instead, he mobilized 318 trained men, pursued the raiders hundreds of miles, defeated them by night, and freed Lot and the other captives. Returning home, Abraham was met and blessed by Melchizedek, king-priest of Salem, to whom he humbly gave a tenth of the spoils. The account shows that this was, in the Bible's own words, one of "the Wars of Jehovah" — not a personal vendetta, but righteous action backed by God.

Abraham and his men riding on camels and charging across a moonlit landscape.
Abraham mobilized 318 trained men from his own household to rescue Lot — a huge, costly undertaking. It reminds me that protecting family and fellow believers sometimes calls for real sacrifice and decisive action, not just good intentions.
A field of date palm trees and other vegetation near the region of ancient Sodom.
The lush vegetation shown here helps explain why Lot was drawn to that area — it looked like the best choice materially. It's a visual reminder that the most attractive option isn't always the safest spiritually.
Abraham and his men giving Melchizedek spoils from the war.
Abraham didn't keep the credit or the spoils for himself — he honored Melchizedek, a priest of Jehovah, with a tenth of the best. This models humility after a hard-won victory.

6. Reflect on the Lessons

1. Jacob felt responsible for each of Laban's sheep. Gen 31:38-40 How can Christian shepherds today show even greater concern for Jehovah's sheep — their brothers and sisters?

“38 During these 20 years that I have been with you, your sheep and your goats never miscarried, and I never ate the rams of your flock. 39 I did not bring you any animal torn by wild beasts. I would stand the loss of it myself. Whether the animal was stolen by day or was stolen by night, you would demand compensation from me. 40 By day the heat consumed me, and the cold by night, and sleep would flee from my eyes.”
📌 Personal application: Jacob's tireless, personal watch-care over animals that weren't even his own is a good standard for how elders — and really any of us caring for others — should treat responsibilities entrusted to us: as if they truly matter, not just as a task to get through.

2. When Jacob was afraid, he prayed to Jehovah. Gen 32:6-12 What do you learn from Jacob about how to pray?

“6 In time the messengers returned to Jacob, saying: “We met your brother Eʹsau, and he is now on his way to meet you, and there are 400 men with him.” 7 And Jacob became very frightened and anxious.
