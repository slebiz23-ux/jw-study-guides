1. They Asked, but They Did Not Listen

10 min. — Talk · Jeremiah 42–44

Jerusalem has fallen and Governor Gedaliah has been murdered. The small remnant left in Judah, led by army chief Johanan, is terrified of Babylonian reprisal. They come to Jeremiah sounding humble and sincere — “May Jehovah your God tell us the way we should walk.” They even swear to obey “whether good or bad.” Ten days later Jehovah answers clearly: stay in Judah; do not go to Egypt. But because the answer wasn’t what they wanted, they called it a lie and marched to Egypt anyway — dragging Jeremiah and Baruch with them. The talk’s point: asking for direction means nothing if we’ve already made up our minds.

Before fleeing to Egypt, Johanan and the people asked Jeremiah to pray for guidance on their behalf
Jer. 42:1-3 it “Johanan” No. 5
1Then all the army chiefs, and Johanan the son of Kareah, Jezaniah the son of Hoshaiah, and all the people, from the least to the greatest, approached 2and said to Jeremiah the prophet: “Hear, please, our request for favor, and pray in our behalf to Jehovah your God, in behalf of all this remnant, for just a few of many are left, as you can see. 3May Jehovah your God tell us the way we should walk and what we should do.”— Jer. 42:1-3 (NWTsty)

Insight explains that Johanan had actually acted well earlier — he supported Gedaliah, warned him of Ishmael’s plot, and rescued the captives. But he was now driven by fear of the Babylonians. Notice they asked Jehovah’s prophet for “the way we should walk” and then promised, “Whether good or bad, we will obey” (Jer 42:5, 6). On the surface, a model request.

5They replied to Jeremiah: “May Jehovah be a true and faithful witness against us if we do not do exactly as Jehovah your God instructs us through you. 6Whether good or bad, we will obey the voice of Jehovah our God, to whom we are sending you, so that it may go well with us because we obey the voice of Jehovah our God.”— Jer. 42:5, 6 (NWTsty)

Jehovah didn’t just say “no.” He spoke straight to their fear — “Do not be afraid because of the king of Babylon … for I am with you” (Jer 42:11) — and he warned that the very sword and famine they were running from would follow them to Egypt (Jer 42:16). The answer was loving, specific, and impossible to misunderstand.

Their response in verse 2 is revealing: “What you are saying is a lie!” Rather than admit they didn’t like the answer, they attacked the messenger and then blamed Baruch for “inciting” Jeremiah (Jer 43:3). When counsel challenges what our heart already wants, it can be tempting to question the motives of the one who gave it.

2. Spiritual Gems

10 min.
Jer 44:18 — Why could the Israelites not justifiably blame their suffering on the fact that they had stopped “making sacrifices to the Queen of Heaven”? (it “Stubbornness” ¶4) ▶
Jer. 44:17, 18 Isa. 48:4, 5 Jer. 44:21-23 it “Stubbornness” ¶4
From the time we quit making sacrifices to the Queen of Heaven and pouring out drink offerings to her, we have lacked everything and have perished by the sword and by the famine.”— Jer. 44:18 (NWTsty)

Answer: Because Jehovah had given them advance warning. For decades he had sent “all my servants the prophets … again and again” telling them exactly why calamity would come — their idolatry and disobedience (Jer 44:4, 5). Insight explains: “Since Jehovah gives advance warning of his judgment against stubborn ones, the execution of that judgment cannot be attributed to other causes or to a different source.” Jehovah said the same through Isaiah, so that Israel could not credit their idols:

4Because I knew how stubborn you are —That your neck is an iron sinew and your forehead is copper— 5I told you long ago. Before it came about, I caused you to hear it, So that you could not say, ‘My idol did this; My carved image and my metal image commanded this.’— Isa. 48:4, 5 (NWTsty)

Jeremiah set the record straight in verses 21-23: the disaster came “because you have made these sacrifices and because you have sinned against Jehovah.” Their logic was backwards — the “good times” they remembered were the years of Jehovah’s patience, not the Queen of Heaven’s blessing.

It is because you have made these sacrifices and because you have sinned against Jehovah by not obeying the voice of Jehovah and following his law, his statutes, and his reminders that this calamity has come upon you, as is the case today.”— Jer. 44:23 (NWTsty)
💬 Comment you could share:“It’s interesting how stubbornness can twist cause and effect. These people had heard Jehovah’s warnings for years, yet when the disaster came, they blamed it on stopping their idol worship! It reminds me that when I don’t want to change, my heart can find ‘evidence’ for almost anything (Jer 17:9).”
📌 Personal application:If I go through hard times, I want to examine things honestly in the light of the Bible rather than reaching for an explanation that excuses me. “Whoever hardens his heart will fall into calamity” (Prov 28:14).

8. Congregation Bible Study — wcg chap. 12: Caleb — “He Fought Loyally for His God”

30 min. — Walk Courageously With God, chapter 12 · Open chapter →
Chapter opening artwork.
📜 Narrative summary ▶

Caleb was born a slave in Egypt about the time Moses fled. At about 40, he saw Jehovah free Israel through the Ten Plagues and the Red Sea — and he never forgot it. He stayed loyal at Sinai when many rebelled, and he was chosen as one of 12 chieftains to spy out Canaan. After 40 days they returned with a grape cluster so large two men had to carry it. But ten spies “kept on giving the Israelites a bad report,” even claiming the inhabitants descended from the Nephilim. The people panicked and talked of choosing a leader to return to Egypt.

Caleb and Joshua held their ground: “Jehovah is with us. Do not fear them.” The crowd talked of stoning them. Jehovah, deeply offended, decreed that the faithless generation would die in the wilderness — but said, “My servant Caleb had a different spirit and kept following after me wholeheartedly.” For 40 years Caleb wandered with the nation he had tried to save, yet he loyally supported Moses and then Joshua. At 85, he asked for Hebron — the very place the faithless spies said was impossible to conquer — saying, “Jehovah will be with me, and I will drive them out.” He took it, “and the land had rest from war.”

Caleb stood up for Jehovah against a crowd of hostile and faithless Israelites. (Caleb shows produce from the Promised Land; Joshua, Moses, and Aaron stand nearby as some in the crowd become angry.)
🖼️ What the picture teachesLook at the faces in the crowd — anger, fear, folded arms. Caleb is outnumbered, yet he’s holding up the evidence of Jehovah’s goodness: the fruit of the land. Courage here isn’t loud defiance; it’s pointing people back to what Jehovah has already shown. When we’re the only one at school or work defending a Bible standard, we too can calmly point to the good fruits of living Jehovah’s way.

🔎 Dig Deeper

1. Why can we say that Numbers 13:23 does not exaggerate the produce of the Promised Land? (w06 6/15 16 ¶1-2) ▶
Num. 13:23, 24 w06 6/15 16 ¶1-2
When they came to the Valley of Eshcol, there they cut down a branch with one cluster of grapes, which two of the men had to carry on a bar, as well as some of the pomegranates and figs.— Num. 13:23 (NWTsty)

Answer: The area near Hebron was famous for its grapes. The spies named it “the torrent valley of Eshcol,” or “Cluster of Grapes.” Centuries later, a 19th-century visitor reported that Eshcol was “still clad with vines, and the grapes are the finest and largest in Palestine.” Much of the land produced fine grapes in Bible times, and Egyptian records show that the Pharaohs imported wine from Canaan — so the account fits the facts.

2. Was the Promised Land really abundant with “milk and honey,” as Caleb and Joshua described it? (Num. 14:8; w11 3/1 15) ▶
Num. 14:8 w11 3/1 15
If Jehovah is pleased with us, he will certainly bring us into this land and give it to us, a land that is flowing with milk and honey.— Num. 14:8 (NWTsty)

Answer: Yes. Israel’s cattle, sheep, and goats supplied plenty of milk. And archaeology now supports the “honey”: at Tel Rehov, excavators found the earliest apiary yet uncovered in the ancient Near East — more than 30 clay beehives in three rows, with perhaps 100 in all, dating to the 10th–early 9th centuries B.C.E. Scholars estimate “as much as half a ton of honey” could have been harvested yearly. Beekeeping was evidently a well-developed industry.

Picture A: Clay beehives in Israel dating from the tenth century to the early ninth century B.C.E. — Institute of Archaeology/Hebrew University © Tel Rehov Excavations
🖼️ What the picture teachesRows of ordinary clay cylinders quietly confirm a phrase Jehovah used centuries earlier. Caleb and Joshua weren’t exaggerating to win an argument — they told the truth about Jehovah’s gift. Discoveries like this strengthen our confidence that the Bible’s details can be trusted, which helps us speak with conviction in the ministry.
3. When Moses sent out the 12 spies from Kadesh, how close were the Israelites to the Promised Land? (w04 10/15 17 ¶11-12) ▶
Deut. 1:2 Heb. 4:11 w04 10/15 17 ¶11-12
It is 11 days from Horeb to Kadesh-barnea by way of Mount Seir.— Deut. 1:2 (NWTsty)

Answer: Very close. They had traveled from Egypt to Mount Sinai and then about 170 miles (270 km) to Kadesh. From Kadesh it was under 60 miles (95 km) to Beer-sheba, a southern edge of the Promised Land, and only about 10 miles (16 km) to Beer-lahai-roi, where Isaac and Rebekah once lived. In the words of the article, “they were as if on the doorstep of the Promised Land.” Their lack of faith turned a short trip into 40 years of wandering.

Picture B: The Israelites’ very long journey from Egypt to Kadesh via Mount Sinai, compared with the relatively short distance from Kadesh to Beer-sheba and Hebron.
🖼️ What the picture teachesThe map makes the tragedy visible: the hardest part of the journey was already behind them. The article applies this to us — we too are on the threshold of the promised Paradise. Now is not the time to lose faith; “let us therefore do our utmost to enter into that rest.”
Let us therefore do our utmost to enter into that rest, so that no one may fall into the same pattern of disobedience.— Heb. 4:11 (NWTsty)

Num. 14:24 Josh. 14:10, 11 w06 10/1 18 ¶11

Answer: Caleb (with Joshua) survived the 40-year trek, just as Jehovah promised (Num 14:30; 32:11, 12). He had an active role in the six-year conquest of Canaan. He enjoyed remarkable life and health — “as strong as on the day Moses sent me out” at 85 — and received Hebron as his personal inheritance. As the article says, his faith grew as he “tasted” Jehovah’s goodness (Ps 34:8). “How Jehovah rewards those who faithfully and courageously serve him!”

Caleb spent 40 years in the wilderness for a sin he didn’t commit, yet he didn’t grow bitter. He kept supporting Moses and stayed ready for Jehovah’s timing. That helps me keep serving joyfully when life feels unfair.”

🌄 Meditate on the Bigger Picture

What does this teach us about Jehovah? ▶
Num. 14:24 Num. 32:11, 12 Num. 14:11

Jehovah notices individuals. Even while an entire nation rejected him, he singled out “my servant Caleb” and described his heart: “a different spirit … wholeheartedly.” He is also patient yet just: he forgave the people according to Moses’ request (Num 14:20) but did not let faithlessness go unanswered. And he keeps his promises to the letter — Caleb himself said, “Just as he promised, Jehovah has preserved me alive these 45 years.”

11‘The men who came up out of Egypt from 20 years old and up will not see the land of which I have sworn to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, because they have not followed me wholeheartedly— 12except Caleb the son of Jephunneh the Kenizzite and Joshua the son of Nun, because they have followed Jehovah wholeheartedly.’— Num. 32:11, 12 (NWTsty)
🙏 Getting to know Jehovah:“I’m touched that Jehovah calls Caleb ‘my servant’ in the middle of one of the saddest chapters in Israel’s history. It reminds me that Jehovah never loses sight of the loyal ones, even when they’re outnumbered. He sees my quiet faithfulness too.”
Are there any points from this lesson we can use in the ministry or our daily life? ▶

The book’s own wrap-up questions also ask, “How does this account tie in with Jehovah’s purposes?” and “What would I like to ask Caleb in the resurrection?” Points you might use:

📌 Ministry:Caleb’s report was honest and positive: the land was “very, very good.” In the ministry we speak about the coming Paradise with the same conviction. And like Caleb, we don’t let discouraging voices — even a majority — weaken our message (Num 14:7-9).
📌 Daily life:We are “on the doorstep” of the promised new world (w04 10/15). Caleb’s generation lost their inheritance by losing faith at the last stage. Hebrews 3:12 warns against “a wicked heart lacking faith.” Every day I want to keep following Jehovah wholeheartedly, right to the end.
Beware, brothers, for fear there should ever develop in any one of you a wicked heart lacking faith by drawing away from the living God;— Heb. 3:12 (NWTsty)
💬 Purpose / resurrection:“Jehovah’s promise to Abraham about the land came true through people like Caleb (Josh 14:9). I’d love to ask Caleb in the resurrection what kept him going during those 40 years — and what it felt like to finally walk into Hebron.”
